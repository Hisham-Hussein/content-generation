# Sweep B — Sentence & block structure (Strunk Rules 12, 14–18, adapted 8/9)

Run this sweep after Sweep A, over the blocks Sweep A exposed as structurally weak.
Sweep A cuts words; Sweep B rearranges what's left so each line lands. Same lens:
fat-vs-substance, voice profile wins, hook untouched.

---

## Rule 12 — Use definite, specific, concrete language

Strunk: "Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to
the abstract. The surest way of arousing and holding the attention of the reader is by
being specific, definite, and concrete." His pairs:

> A period of unfavorable weather set in. → It rained every day for a week.
> He showed satisfaction as he took possession of his well-earned reward. → He grinned as he pocketed the coin.

And Spencer's, which he quotes because abstraction taxes the reader ("we do not think
in generals, but in particulars"):

> In proportion as the manners, customs, and amusements of a nation are cruel and
> barbarous, the regulations of their penal code will be severe.
> → In proportion as men delight in battles, bull-fights, and combats of gladiators,
> will they punish by hanging, burning, and the rack.

Caption pairs:

> "AI can dramatically improve your operations."
> → "AI cut their invoice-processing time from 4 days to 20 minutes."

> "This creates significant inefficiencies across the organization."
> → "Every contract waits three days for one person's signature."

This is the rule that turns tightening into *sharpening*: the concrete version is often
LONGER than the vague one and still wins — swap it in, then pay for it with Sweep A cuts
elsewhere. When the KI holds a real number or named case that the caption vagued away,
flag it (this borders drill Step 6b depth work — propose, don't silently expand).

## Rule 14 — Avoid a succession of loose sentences

Strunk's target: a run of two-clause sentences all glued with and/but/who/which —
"mechanical symmetry and sing-song." His fix: recast enough of them to break the
monotony — simple sentences, semicolons, periodic sentences — "whichever best represent
the real relations of the thought."

Caption adaptation: the disease shows up as every line-block being the same shape —
same length, same "X, but Y" build. The voice profile's staccato fragments are the
natural cure; this rule CONVERGES with the voice:

> "We built the pilot, and the team started using it within a week. The results were
> strong, but adoption stalled at the managers. We ran workshops, and things improved."
> → "We built the pilot. The team was using it within a week.
> Then adoption stalled. At the managers.
> Workshops fixed it."

Vary block length deliberately: a one-word line after two full lines is rhythm, not error.

## Rule 15 — Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form (parallelism)

Strunk: "Expressions of similar content and function should be outwardly similar. The
likeness of form enables the reader to recognize more readily the likeness of content."
The unskillful writer breaks it "from a mistaken belief that he should constantly vary
the form of his expressions."

> Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method, while now the laboratory method
> is employed. → Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method; now it is taught
> by the laboratory method.

> It was both a long ceremony and very tedious. → The ceremony was both long and tedious.
> A time not for words, but action. → A time not for words, but for action.

Caption application — this rule owns the ✅/↳/1️⃣ lists. Every item in one list starts
with the same part of speech and shape:

> ✅ Automating the intake
> ✅ You should delegate approvals
> ✅ Faster reporting
> → ✅ Automate the intake
> ✅ Delegate the approvals
> ✅ Speed up the reporting

Repeat or drop the leading article/preposition consistently across a series ("in spring,
summer, or winter" — never "in spring, summer, or in winter"). Correlatives (both/and,
either/or, first/second) take the same construction on both sides.

Note the boundary with the humanizer: forced rule-of-three is a tell; parallel FORM
across however many items the content truly has (2, 4, 5…) is craft. Parallelism does
not mean padding to three.

## Rule 16 — Keep related words together

Strunk: "The position of the words in a sentence is the principal means of showing their
relationship." Subject and verb stay close; the interrupting phrase moves to the front;
the modifier sits next to the word it modifies.

> Wordsworth, in the fifth book of The Excursion, gives a minute description of this
> church. → In the fifth book of The Excursion, Wordsworth gives a minute description
> of this church.

> He only found two mistakes. → He found only two mistakes.
> All the members were not present. → Not all the members were present.

Caption pair:

> "Founders, after years of building the muscle of deciding everything, struggle to let go."
> → "After years of deciding everything, founders struggle to let go."

Short caption lines rarely violate this — but when a line reads twice before it parses,
this is usually why.

## Rule 17 — In summaries, keep to one tense

Caption adaptation (compressed — the drama-summary detail doesn't apply): pick a tense
for a story beat and hold it. A case study told in past ("L'Oréal rolled out…") doesn't
flip to present mid-block ("…and the team sees 99.9%"). Shifting tense "gives the
appearance of uncertainty and irresolution." Also from Strunk's expansion: when
relaying a source's argument, signal it ONCE — don't re-attribute line by line
("the talk says… the speaker adds… he also notes"). Attribute once, then state.

## Rule 18 — Place the emphatic words at the end

Strunk: "The proper place for the word or group of words which the writer desires to
make most prominent is usually the end." The end-slot belongs to the NEW element, the
logical predicate:

> Humanity has hardly advanced in fortitude since that time, though it has advanced in
> many other ways. → Humanity, since that time, has advanced in many other ways, but it
> has hardly advanced in fortitude.

The other emphatic slot is the beginning ("Deceit or treachery he could never forgive").
The middle is where words go to be ignored.

**Caption adaptation — the line break IS the emphasis position.** In a caption, every
line-block ends in white space, so every block-final word sits in a Rule 18 power slot.
End each block on its power word, not on a qualifier:

> "You'll get your evenings back, in most cases, within a quarter or so."
> → "Within a quarter, most founders get their evenings back."

> "The bottleneck isn't your team. It's the founder, unfortunately."
> → "The bottleneck isn't your team. It's you."

This principle "applies equally to the words of a sentence, to the sentences of a
paragraph, and to the paragraphs of a composition" — so also check: does the last
line-block before the CTA carry the post's strongest beat?

## Rules 8/9 adapted — the line-block is the paragraph

Strunk's paragraph rules, translated to caption anatomy (his prose-paragraph advice
does NOT apply literally — one-line paragraphs are the voice, not a violation):

- **One topic per line-block** (R8: "one paragraph to each topic"). A block holding two
  ideas splits; a block restating the previous block is fat — delete it.
- **The block's first line does the topic-sentence job** (R9): a reader skimming only
  first lines should still follow the argument.
- **End in conformity with the beginning** (R9): the block's last line emphasizes or
  pays off its first — "ending with a digression, or with an unimportant detail, is
  particularly to be avoided." A block that trails off gets its tail cut (which also
  serves the char ceiling).
- The blank line between blocks is Strunk's "signal that a new step in the development
  of the subject has been reached." If two adjacent blocks are one step, merge them.
