# Usage rules — punctuation & grammar essentials (Strunk Rules 1–7)

Consult-on-demand file: read when the Step 5 checklist surfaces a punctuation or
grammar doubt, not as a routine pass. Strunk: Rules 3–6 "cover the most important
principles in the punctuation of ordinary sentences."

## Rule 1 — Possessive singular: add 's

"Charles's friend", "the witch's malice" — whatever the final consonant. Pronominal
possessives (hers, its, theirs, yours) take no apostrophe. In captions the live trap is
its/it's.

## Rule 2 — Serial comma

In a series of three or more with one conjunction, comma after each term except the
last: "people, processes, and products". House style follows Strunk: keep the Oxford
comma.

## Rule 3 — Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas

"The best way to see a country, unless you are pressed for time, is to travel on foot."
The unbreakable half of the rule: **never one comma without the other** — "Marjorie's
husband, Colonel Nelson paid us a visit" is indefensible. If the interruption is slight,
both commas may go; a pair or nothing.

Non-restrictive clauses take the commas ("The audience, which had at first been
indifferent, became interested"); restrictive ones don't ("The candidate who best meets
these requirements will obtain the place") — the clause that IDENTIFIES gets no commas.

Caption note: since em dashes are banned, parenthetic commas (or a separate short
sentence) are the sanctioned way to hold an aside. Often the better fix is Strunk's
implicit one: the aside is fat — delete it.

## Rule 4 — Comma before a conjunction introducing a co-ordinate clause

"The situation is perilous, but there is still one chance of escape." When both clauses
share one stated subject and the connective is "and", drop the comma ("He has had
several years' experience and is thoroughly competent"); with "but", keep it ("I have
heard his arguments, but am still unconvinced").

Strunk's deeper point: "and" is the least specific connective. If the real relation is
cause or concession, say so ("As the records disappeared…", "Although the situation is
perilous…") — or in caption register, break into two blocks and let the break carry
the logic.

## Rule 5 — Do not join independent clauses by a comma

Comma splice: "Stevenson's romances are entertaining, they are full of exciting
adventures" — wrong. Semicolon, or two sentences. Captions prefer the period: two short
sentences beat a semicolon on a phone screen. The adverb trap: "besides", "then",
"therefore", "thus" between clauses still need more than a comma.

Strunk's exception fits the voice: very short clauses alike in form may take the comma —
"Man proposes, God disposes." A deliberate rhythmic pair may stand.

## Rule 6 — Do not break sentences in two

Don't use a period where a comma belongs: "He was an interesting talker. A man who had
traveled all over the world." — blunder.

**The carve-out that protects the voice:** "It is permissible to make an emphatic word
or expression serve the purpose of a sentence: 'Again and again he called out. No
reply.'" — provided "the emphasis is warranted." Strunk himself sanctions the emphatic
fragment. The test for any caption fragment: does it land a beat (keep) or is it just a
clause that lost its sentence (rejoin)?

## Rule 7 — Opening participial phrase must refer to the grammatical subject

"Walking slowly down the road, he saw a woman…" — the walker is "he". Violations are
often ludicrous: "Being in a dilapidated condition, I was able to buy the house very
cheap." / "Wondering irresolutely what to do next, the clock struck twelve."

Caption trap — the dangling opener before "we":

> "After rolling AI into a dozen firms, the pattern is obvious."
> → "After rolling AI into a dozen firms, we see the same pattern every time."
> (or: "We've rolled AI into a dozen firms. Same pattern every time.")
